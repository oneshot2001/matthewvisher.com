Agent Action Receipts

Before an agent's output moves something in the world or becomes evidence, what has to be provable about it?

The problem in the field

I spent a career handing evidence to people who had every reason to doubt it. Chain of custody was the answer: who had it, when, and what they did with it, written down in a way a stranger could check. Agents are now doing things that used to need a person with a badge. Unlocking a door. Moving a camera. Tagging a clip that ends up in a case file. The question I keep coming back to is what must be independently provable about what an agent saw, inferred, was authorized to do, and actually did, before its output triggers physical-world action or becomes evidence.

Why the usual answers fail

The audit trail lives in the platform that did the work, so a compromised or careless platform grades its own homework. Reading the trail often needs an account and a license from the vendor. And JSON logs are easy to write and hard to sign, because two encoders will serialize the same record two ways.

What I built

A small wire spec and a reference verifier. Receipts are deterministic CBOR with a detached COSE_Sign1 signature (ES256), closed schemas, and normative CDDL. The verifier, pyref, is Python 3.11 standard library only. No network, no wall clock, no dependencies. Verifying receipts is free, forever, offline, with no account and no license from anyone. The conformance vectors are byte-pinned known-answer tests generated by a second, clean-room implementation in TypeScript, which I also wrote, and the threat model, v0.1, lists 30 findings. The reference producer is onvif-mcp. Since October 12 it writes v0.2.3. All 22 live bundles it has written since, snapshots from my three lab cameras, verify as conformant in pyref v0.2.5. Its older receipts stay in the August pre-release format they were signed in.

How it works

A producer signs each receipt and links it to the previous one. The verifier runs twenty checks in a fixed order, from the size of the bundle to the signed verdict, and names the first one that fails. This is a test bundle from the repo, so you can run it yourself:

$ python3 -B -m pyref verify kats/positive/bundle-valid-subset.cbor --at 1735689800

What that gives you: attribution to a signer, tamper evidence after emission, and tamper evidence over the declared contents of an epoch. What it does not give you: it can't stop a compromised signer from lying before it signs, it can't prove an inference was correct, and it doesn't establish legal compliance with anything. I'd rather say that here than have someone find out in a hearing.

Where it stands

A stable experimental spec, not an adopted standard. Both implementations are mine, so nobody independent has built one yet, and that gap matters, because the best bug I've found so far came from a cross-check. The reference CBOR encoder wrote every unsigned integer between 2^24 and 2^32 wrong. That range covers every real Unix timestamp. All 188 fixtures stayed green because they came from the same encoder, and pyref read those bytes back the same way. It took a demo run with a real, current timestamp: pyref decoded the bundle and disagreed with the time it was handed. One implementation agreeing with itself proves nothing. An October 8 audit found the two still differed on 40 of 232 test fixtures, signing different verdicts, and on one of those the result itself was different. v0.2.5 closed that. Given the same inputs and the same verifier identity, every one of the 478 fixtures in the suite now gets the same signed verdict from both, byte for byte. That claim stops at what the fixtures pin. A case nobody wrote a fixture for isn't covered, and I don't claim it.

What's next

v0.2.5 is tagged, as of October 19. Since v0.2.0 in September there have been five numbered releases, each closing a hole a review found: the signed verdict now commits to the prior history it was judged against (v0.2.1), every signed object has to be signed by the key it names (v0.2.2), and a receipt can declare where an action's inputs came from, such as a camera, the operator or a third party, which the verifier passes on as a stated limit rather than a fact (v0.2.3). v0.2.4 closed three more: a permission can only be used by the enforcement point it was issued to, the piece that actually sends the command; a key change has to be signed by whoever issued the old key, and the key changes a bundle carries for one site have to be in order; and a receipt signed before its key was revoked still verifies, while one signed after does not. v0.2.5 changed nothing on the wire. It made the two verifiers agree. The conformance suite runs in CI on every change. The part of what's left that I can't do myself is one external producer emitting receipts that pyref verifies without my help. That external run, or an acknowledgement from the people writing the adjacent standards, is the signal I'm watching for. The wire may still change in response to it.
